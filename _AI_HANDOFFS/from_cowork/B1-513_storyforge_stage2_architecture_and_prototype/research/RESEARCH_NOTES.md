# RESEARCH_NOTES — StoryForge "Inspiration" Wizard (b1-513)

**Purpose.** Evidence base for a guided wizard that helps residency applicants (US and international medical graduates) escape autobiographical tunnel vision and surface relatable, interview-usable stories from their whole lives — not just stereotypical leadership/conflict/failure set pieces.

**Method.** Web research (2026-08-07) across narrative medicine, reflective medical education, oral history practice, life-story and qualitative interviewing, autobiographical-memory science, behavioral interviewing, memoir/storytelling craft, human-centered design interviewing, and closeness-generating conversation research. Key claims verified across at least two independent sources where possible. No proprietary question banks were copied; only method-level principles were extracted.

**Provenance vocabulary used below.**
- *adapted* — MissionMed content applies a method-level structure from the source (never verbatim text or question lists).
- *inspired* — the source's findings/philosophy informed design choices; no structural borrowing.
- *independently authored (context only)* — source establishes the target use-case; no content derived from it.

## A. Narrative medicine & reflective medical education

### 1. Columbia University — Narrative Medicine (Rita Charon)
- **Source:** Columbia University Dept. of Medical Humanities and Ethics, "The Principles and Practice of Narrative Medicine" — https://www.mhe.cuimc.columbia.edu/narrative-medicine/education-and-narrative-medicine/principles-and-practice-narrative-medicine ; see also R. Charon, "Narrative Medicine: Attention, Representation, Affiliation" (2005) — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236705367_Narrative_Medicine_Attention_Representation_Affiliation
- **Credibility:** Founding academic program of the field; Charon (MD, PhD) originated the discipline at Columbia.
- **Insight:** Narrative medicine's triad — attention (skilled receiving of accounts), representation (writing/telling an experience to *discover* its meaning), affiliation (connection built through shared stories). The act of putting an experience into narrative form is itself how meaning becomes visible; clinicians and patients alike understand their experience better after telling it.
- **Use:** *inspired* — grounds the wizard's core claim that telling a story is how an applicant discovers what it means, and that clinical stories are about receiving others' stories, not only one's own heroics.

### 2. BMC Medical Education — Systematic scoping review of reflective writing
- **Source:** "A systematic scoping review of reflective writing in medical education," BMC Medical Education (2022) — https://bmcmededuc.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12909-022-03924-4
- **Credibility:** Peer-reviewed systematic scoping review in an established medical-education journal.
- **Insight:** Reflective writing improves learning, self-awareness, and professional identity formation — but only reliably when it is *guided*: frameworks, prompts, examples of expected depth, and personalized, supportive feedback in a safe environment. Unstructured "just reflect" instructions underperform.
- **Use:** *inspired* — justifies scaffolding (structured prompts + follow-ups) over a blank-page "tell us your story" box.

### 3. AMEE Guide No. 44 (Sandars) — The use of reflection in medical education
- **Source:** J. Sandars, "The use of reflection in medical education: AMEE Guide No. 44," Medical Teacher 31(8), 2009 — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01421590903050374 (open PDF: https://medicine.usask.ca/documents/faculty-affairs/workshops/ReflectionMEdicalEducation.pdf)
- **Credibility:** AMEE guides are the standard reference series in international medical education.
- **Insight:** Reflection is a deliberate, structured process (before/during/after experiences) that supports professional development and therapeutic relationships; learners need guidance, a supportive mentor/facilitator, and psychological safety for reflection to be more than description. Corroborates source 2.
- **Use:** *inspired* — supports staged deepening (recall the scene first, interpret meaning later) and a non-judgmental tone.

### 4. AAMC — Advisor Corner: Crafting Your Personal Statement
- **Source:** AAMC Students & Residents, "Advisor Corner: Crafting Your Personal Statement" — https://students-residents.aamc.org/applying-medical-school/advisor-corner-crafting-your-personal-statement
- **Credibility:** Official US medical-education association guidance; advisors quoted are prehealth deans/advisors.
- **Insight:** Advisors recommend capturing concrete moments (even journaling them as they happen) and warn that a story without *change* is "a recitation of facts"; reflection on what you learned and how you changed matters more than a catchy theme. Chasing uniqueness produces artificial writing.
- **Use:** *independently authored (context only)* — defines what a "usable" output story must contain (specific moment + change/insight), which the `interviewUse` field encodes.

### 5. American Medical Association — Behavioral questions in residency interviews
- **Source:** AMA, "Residency interviews: How to answer behavioral questions like a pro" — https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-students/preparing-residency/residency-interviews-how-answer-behavioral-questions-pro ; see also AAMC, "Developing Structured Residency and Fellowship Interviews" — https://www.aamc.org/services/admission-interview-foundations/developing-structured-residency-interviews
- **Credibility:** Official AMA applicant guidance; AAMC page documents structured-interview practice in residency selection.
- **Insight:** Programs increasingly use structured "tell me about a time…" behavioral questions probing teamwork, stress, feedback, and adaptability; applicants need a supply of *specific* examples showing collaboration ("we" language), self-awareness, and growth — not rehearsed hero narratives.
- **Use:** *independently authored (context only)* — defines the downstream target: every wizard prompt should be able to feed a behavioral answer or personal-statement anecdote.

## B. Oral history & folklife interviewing

### 6. StoryCorps — Tips for a Great Conversation / Great Questions practice
- **Source:** StoryCorps, "Tips for a Great Conversation" — https://storycorps.org/participate/tips-for-a-great-conversation/ ; "Great Questions" (practice, not copied) — https://storycorps.org/participate/great-questions/
- **Credibility:** The largest US oral-history nonprofit; partner of the Library of Congress American Folklife Center.
- **Insight:** Method principles: open invitations ("Tell me about…", "What was it like when…") beat yes/no questions; questions are a guide, not a script; start with easy warm-ups before deeper territory; prompt for sensory specifics (sounds, smells, images); use simple follow-ups ("And then what happened?", "How did that make you feel?"); reserve reflective/legacy questions for the end.
- **Use:** *inspired* — method-level only (question phrasing style, warm-up-first ordering, follow-up pattern). **No StoryCorps questions were reproduced.**

### 7. Smithsonian Institution — Folklife and Oral History Interviewing Guide (Marjorie Hunt)
- **Source:** Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage, "The Smithsonian Folklife and Oral History Interviewing Guide" — https://folklife-media.si.edu/docs/folklife/interviewing_guide/InterviewingGuide.pdf (mirror consulted: https://museumonmainstreet.org/sites/default/files/Smithsonian%20oral%20history%20guide.pdf)
- **Credibility:** Smithsonian-published field guide by a folklife curator; widely used in schools and museums.
- **Insight:** Treats ordinary people as "living links in the historical chain": everyday domains — foodways, celebrations, occupational skill, family sayings, traditions — are where identity actually lives. Tangible anchors (photos, objects, places) unlock richer recollection, and "How/What" questions plus "Can you give me an example?" follow-ups turn generalities into scenes.
- **Use:** *adapted* (method level) — the wizard's "memory territories" (food, celebrations, work, family sayings, objects) directly apply this domain map; all question text is original.

### 8. Baylor University — Institute for Oral History, Introduction to Oral History
- **Source:** Baylor University Institute for Oral History, "Introduction to Oral History" manual — https://library.web.baylor.edu/sites/g/files/ecbvkj1806/files/2024-12/intro_manual_2016.pdf ; workshop hub: https://library.web.baylor.edu/visit/institute-oral-history/workshops
- **Credibility:** One of the longest-running university oral-history institutes in the US; its manual is a standard training text.
- **Insight:** Ask open-ended, *neutral* (non-leading) questions, strictly one question at a time; probe with restatement ("You said X — tell me more"); embrace silence after an answer, because pauses surface additional memories; build rapport first; the narrator retains authority and autonomy over their story (consent, right to decline).
- **Use:** *adapted* (method level) — one-question-at-a-time flow, follow-up probe design, and narrator-autonomy/skip principles.

### 9. Library of Congress — Veterans History Project field kit
- **Source:** Library of Congress, "Veterans History Project Field Kit" — https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/veterans-history-project/documents/vhp-field-kit-complete.pdf ; program guide: https://guides.loc.gov/veterans-history
- **Credibility:** Congressionally mandated Library of Congress program; its kit trains lay interviewers nationwide.
- **Insight:** Demonstrates that untrained interviewers reliably elicit vivid life stories when given a simple chronological scaffold, open-ended prompts about everyday specifics (daily routines, friendships, food, letters home — not only combat), and explicit permission for narrators to decline any topic. Ordinary-life questions are treated as historically valuable, and difficult topics are approached only by invitation.
- **Use:** *inspired* — validates lay-interviewer scaffolding, ordinary-detail questions, and decline-freely norms for sensitive areas.

## C. Life-story & qualitative interviewing

### 10. Dan McAdams — The Life Story Interview (Foley Center, Northwestern)
- **Source:** D. P. McAdams, "The Life Story Interview – II" (2007), Foley Center for the Study of Lives, Northwestern University — https://cpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/4/3901/files/2020/11/The-Life-Story-Interview-II-2007.pdf ; lab: https://sites.northwestern.edu/danmcadams/
- **Credibility:** The canonical research protocol in narrative-identity psychology; hundreds of studies use it.
- **Insight:** Identity is elicited through *key scenes*, not trait questions: for each scene the protocol asks what happened, when and where, who was involved, what you were thinking and feeling, and what the scene says about you. Scene types go beyond high/low points — turning points, vivid ordinary adult memories, wisdom moments — and the same detail scaffold is applied to every scene.
- **Use:** *adapted* (method-level scaffold only) — the wizard's follow-up pattern (scene → sensation/thought/feeling → meaning) applies McAdams' scene-detail structure. **The protocol itself was not reproduced.**

### 11. Jefferson Singer & Peter Salovey — Self-defining memories
- **Source:** J. A. Singer, P. Salovey, *The Remembered Self* (1993) — https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Remembered_Self.html?id=CcV-AAAAMAAJ ; Singer, Blagov, Berry & Oost, "Self-Defining Memories, Scripts, and the Life Story," Journal of Personality (2013) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jopy.12005
- **Credibility:** Foundational peer-reviewed research program on memory and personality (Salovey later president of Yale).
- **Insight:** Self-defining memories are vivid, emotionally intense, repeatedly recalled episodes linked to enduring concerns — and crucially, well-being correlates with *meaning-making from specific memories*, not with the events' objective magnitude. Small, specific episodes narrated with insight do the identity work.
- **Use:** *inspired* — supports privileging ordinary-but-vivid moments over "impressive" peak events, and pairing each memory with a meaning question.

### 12. Robert Butler — Life review (supporting source)
- **Source:** R. N. Butler, "The Life Review: An Interpretation of Reminiscence in the Aged," Psychiatry 26(1), 1963 — https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-662-38534-0_20
- **Credibility:** Classic, heavily cited psychiatry paper; founded reminiscence/life-review practice.
- **Insight:** Reviewing one's past is a naturally occurring, constructive process rather than mere nostalgia; structured reminiscence supports integration of identity. (Second source for the legitimacy of guided reminiscence alongside 10 and 11.)
- **Use:** *inspired* — frames the wizard as a normal, healthy activity, not therapy; reinforces the no-diagnosis-framing rule.

## D. Autobiographical-memory science

### 13. Crovitz & Schiffman — the Galton–Crovitz cue-word method
- **Source:** H. F. Crovitz & H. Schiffman, "Frequency of episodic memories as a function of their age," Bulletin of the Psychonomic Society 4 (1974); method summary — https://loterre.istex.fr/P66/en/page/-D6LFMB15-2 ; secondary explainer — https://psychologyfor.com/crovitz-technique-what-it-is-and-how-it-is-used-in-mental-health/
- **Credibility:** The standard laboratory paradigm (revival of Galton 1879) used across autobiographical-memory research for 50 years.
- **Insight:** A single concrete cue word ("bread," "river," "letter") reliably triggers retrieval of a specific personal episode; concrete, imageable cues outperform abstract ones. Memory search is cue-driven — people cannot "browse" their past, but a good cue makes retrieval nearly automatic.
- **Use:** *adapted* (method level) — the entire territory-cue design (food, objects, places, weather, first jobs) is a conversational application of cue-word retrieval.

### 14. Koppel & Rubin — the reminiscence bump
- **Source:** J. Koppel & D. C. Rubin, "Recent Advances in Understanding the Reminiscence Bump," Current Directions in Psychological Science 25(2), 2016 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0963721416631955 ; corroborating: Glück & Bluck, "Looking back across the life span: A life story account of the reminiscence bump," Memory & Cognition (2007) — https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/Bump.Glueck.Bluck_.2007.pdf
- **Credibility:** Rubin is the leading researcher on autobiographical-memory distribution; peer-reviewed review article.
- **Insight:** Adults disproportionately recall events from roughly ages 10–30 (the reminiscence bump), and the bump's location shifts with the cueing method — word cues surface earlier, more mundane memories, while "important memory" requests pull later, life-script events. Applicants' richest material sits exactly in the school/university/early-career years — and asking for "important" memories biases toward clichéd life-script events. Caveat: the bump itself is documented in older adults recalling their youth; for 25–35-year-old applicants the design conclusion rests on the cue-dependence and life-script findings rather than on the bump per se.
- **Use:** *inspired* — justifies heavy coverage of childhood, school, first jobs, and university territories, and avoiding "most important" phrasings.

### 15. Raes, Williams & Hermans — Memory Specificity Training (MEST)
- **Source:** F. Raes, J. M. G. Williams, D. Hermans, "Reducing overgeneral autobiographical memory retrieval…," J. Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry 40 (2009) — https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/Raes%20et%20al,%20J%20Beh%20Ther%20Exp%20Psychiatry%2040,%202009.pdf ; replication in older adults — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13825585.2017.1365815
- **Credibility:** Peer-reviewed clinical research by leading memory researchers; replicated in multiple populations.
- **Insight:** People often retrieve *categorical* summaries ("times I studied hard") instead of single events; practicing retrieval of one specific event per cue — pushing toward sensory and contextual detail — measurably improves specificity, and gains transfer to problem solving. Specificity is a trainable skill, and "one particular time" phrasing plus detail probes is the training mechanism.
- **Use:** *inspired* — every prompt asks for one particular occasion; follow-ups push toward sensory/contextual detail.

### 16. Madore & Schacter — Episodic specificity induction
- **Source:** D. L. Schacter & K. P. Madore, "Remembering the past and imagining the future…," (review incl. Madore & Schacter 2016, QJEP) — https://cpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.harvard.edu/dist/3/137/files/2022/08/schacter-madore-2016.pdf ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17470218.2014.999097
- **Credibility:** Harvard memory lab (Schacter); peer-reviewed, repeatedly replicated paradigm.
- **Insight:** A brief warm-up — mentally picturing one recent event and reporting everything about the setting, people, and actions — boosts the episodic detail of *subsequent* remembering, imagining, and even creative problem solving, with no effect on non-episodic tasks. A single "practice memory" at wizard start is expected to bias the immediately following retrievals toward episodic mode. Caveat: the lab paradigm probes a just-watched video and measures effects on immediately subsequent tasks; session-long carryover is a design extrapolation, not a demonstrated result — which is one reason the warm-up step is an R4 candidate rather than a shipped R3 mechanism.
- **Use:** *inspired* — motivates an opening low-stakes "picture one recent moment" warm-up step and detail-first follow-ups.

### 17. Fisher & Geiselman — The Cognitive Interview
- **Source:** R. P. Fisher & R. E. Geiselman, *Memory-Enhancing Techniques for Investigative Interviewing: The Cognitive Interview* (1992); method summary — https://www.hptc-pro.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/Cognitive-Interview-Method-Fisher-Geiselman.pdf ; overview — https://www.simplypsychology.org/cognitive-interview.html
- **Credibility:** The dominant evidence-based interview protocol in forensic psychology; large replication literature.
- **Insight:** Mental context reinstatement (re-imagining the physical setting, weather, mood) and "report everything, even trivial details" instructions substantially increase accurate recall; trivial details act as retrieval routes to significant ones. Open narrative first, probes second; never leading questions.
- **Use:** *adapted* (method level) — follow-ups that re-place the user in the scene ("Where were you? What could you see/hear?") apply context reinstatement.

## E. Behavioral interviewing, storytelling craft, conversation design

### 18. Tom Janz — Behavior description interviewing (origin of STAR-style practice)
- **Source:** T. Janz, L. Hellervik, D. C. Gilmore, *Behavior Description Interviewing* (1986) — https://archive.org/details/behaviordescript00janz ; university STAR guide example — https://www.saintpeters.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/112/files/2012/07/STAR-Method-Behavioral-Interviews5.pdf
- **Credibility:** The original academic/practitioner text behind behavior-description (behavioral) interviewing; STAR guides are its ubiquitous descendants.
- **Insight:** The premise of behavioral interviewing: the best predictor of future behavior is past behavior *in specific situations* — so interviewers probe for one concrete episode (situation, actions taken, result) rather than hypotheticals or self-descriptions. Applicants therefore need episodes retrievable on demand, pre-processed into situation-action-outcome form.
- **Use:** *inspired* — the `interviewUse` field maps each prompt to the behavioral-answer material it yields; no STAR trademark question lists copied.

### 19. The Moth — Storytelling craft
- **Source:** The Moth, "Storytelling Tips & Tricks" — https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks (also https://www.themoth.org/tell-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks)
- **Credibility:** Premier nonprofit live-storytelling organization; its craft guidance is widely taught.
- **Insight:** A story needs stakes (what you stood to gain or lose), a painted scene the listener is roped into, and an ending as a *changed person* — triumphant, defeated, or befuddled. First-person lived experience beats secondhand accounts; authenticity beats performance. Converges with AAMC's "story without change is recitation" (source 4).
- **Use:** *inspired* — follow-ups nudge toward stakes and change; prompts prefer moments where something shifted.

### 20. IDEO.org — Design Kit interview method / Field Guide to Human-Centered Design
- **Source:** IDEO.org Design Kit, "Interview" method — https://www.designkit.org/methods/interview.html ; *The Field Guide to Human-Centered Design* (2015) — https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/bassjrcomd3701spr2021ol75/files/2021/02/Field-Guide-to-Human-Centered-Design_IDEO_lo.pdf
- **Credibility:** Standard-setting human-centered-design toolkit used across the nonprofit/design sector.
- **Insight:** Start broad (life, values) before narrowing to the target topic; ask for specific stories rather than opinions; what people *say* is one data point — the context and texture around it carries the insight. Environment and concrete artifacts surface truths that direct questions miss.
- **Use:** *inspired* — supports broad-to-narrow session flow and story-not-opinion phrasing.

### 21. Aron et al. — The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness ("36 questions")
- **Source:** A. Aron, E. Melinat, E. N. Aron, R. D. Vallone, R. J. Bator, "The Experimental Generation of Interpersonal Closeness," Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 23(4), 1997 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167297234003 (PDF: https://www.stafforini.com/docs/Aron%20et%20al%20-%20The%20experimental%20generation%20of%20interpersonal%20closeness.pdf)
- **Credibility:** Classic peer-reviewed social-psychology experiment; extensively replicated and applied.
- **Insight:** *Sustained, gradually escalating* personal self-disclosure — three question sets rising in intimacy over 45 minutes — produced closeness that small talk did not; escalation and reciprocity were the active ingredients, not matching or expectations. Depth is reached by ramping, never by opening with the deepest question.
- **Use:** *inspired* — evidence for the Light → Serious → Moving energy ramp and for defaulting sessions to lighter openers. **No Aron questions reused.**

## Synthesized design principles

Each principle cites its supporting sources by number above.

**P1. Cue the episode, not the trait.** Ask "tell me about one particular time / one meal / one afternoon," never "what are your strengths" or "describe an important experience." Memory retrieval is cue-driven; concrete, imageable cues trigger specific episodes almost automatically, while abstract or "important" framings surface clichéd life-script summaries. [13, 14, 15, 10]

**P2. One question at a time, questions as a guide not a script.** Bundled or scripted questioning suppresses narration; single open invitations ("Tell me about…", "What was it like when…") with freedom to wander produce the richest material. [8, 6, 17]

**P3. Warm up episodically before going anywhere deep.** A brief, low-stakes "picture one recent moment in detail" opener measurably increases the episodic detail of everything recalled afterward — build a specificity warm-up into session start. [16, 17, 6]

**P4. Escalate gradually: light → serious → moving.** Depth is produced by ramped, reciprocal disclosure, not by opening with the heaviest question; StoryCorps and oral-history practice independently converge on easy warm-ups first, reflective questions last. [21, 6, 8]

**P5. Light and funny questions are retrieval tools, not filler.** Humor, food, mishaps, and hobbies lower stakes, widen the search set beyond the rehearsed "application self," and reach the everyday domains (foodways, sayings, celebrations, work rituals) where identity actually lives. [7, 6, 14]

**P6. Ordinary moments reveal character better than peak events.** Meaning made from a specific small episode predicts identity insight better than event magnitude; folklife practice treats everyday routines as historically valuable; "vivid ordinary memory" is a core McAdams scene type. [11, 7, 10, 9]

**P7. Probe the scene with a fixed detail scaffold.** After the story surfaces, deepen with: what happened → where/when → who was there → what could you see/hear → what were you thinking and feeling → what does it say about you. Context reinstatement and report-everything probes recover detail; the meaning question converts detail into identity material. [10, 17, 15, 1]

**P8. Trivial details are retrieval routes.** Encourage "even the small stuff" — sensory and contextual minutiae unlock significant memories that direct questioning misses; objects, photos, dishes, and places are legitimate entry points. [17, 7, 13]

**P9. Agency and easy exits are non-negotiable.** The narrator owns the story: skip is always available, emotional questions are invitations with visible exits ("share only what feels comfortable"), and no prompt may demand trauma, grief, or error confession. This is oral-history ethics, not just UX politeness — and safety is a precondition for reflection at all. [8, 9, 2, 3]

**P10. Every story needs stakes and a change.** A usable interview/personal-statement story shows what was at stake and how the teller ended changed — "a story without change is a recitation of facts." Follow-ups should nudge toward the shift, not just the event. [19, 4, 5]

**P11. Target the reminiscence bump — and the whole life before medicine.** Memories cluster in ages ~10–30: school, friendships, first jobs, family life, migration, university. Deliberately cueing these territories counters tunnel vision that starts every story in the hospital. [14, 7, 10]

**P12. Stories about *other people* are first-class material.** Narrative medicine centers receiving others' stories; behavioral interviewers reward "we" language and observed exemplars; questions about mentors, family, colleagues, and patients yield humility and relational insight that self-focused questions cannot. [1, 5, 6, 10]

**P13. Structure and feedback beat blank pages.** Guided prompts with exemplars and staged support outperform unstructured reflection; the wizard should scaffold (territory → episode → detail → meaning) rather than ask for finished narratives. [2, 3, 20]

**P14. Ask for stories, not opinions — and start broad.** Opinion questions get self-theories; story questions get evidence. Begin with the person's broader life before narrowing to medicine, and treat what is said as one data point to be deepened. [20, 6, 18]

## Design notes carried into PROMPT_LIBRARY.json

- **Energy labels kept as specified** ("Serious & Meaningful" / "Fun & Lighthearted" / "Emotional & Moving") — research supports the *ordering* (light first, moving last per P4) more than any relabeling; UI should default the ramp, not rename it.
- **`whoDetail`** added as a filterable sub-dimension with `parent` links (family and someone_else subtypes), per the spec.
- **IMG safety:** prompts avoid US-centric anchors (no prom, Thanksgiving, Little League, SAT references); migration/"starting over" prompts are framed as opportunity, not deficit; family prompts assume no particular family structure.
- **Clinical ethics:** patient-related prompts require de-identification in the question text itself and never ask for error/harm confession; "moving" clinical prompts carry explicit exits (P9).
- **Copyright hygiene:** all 81 questions are MissionMed-original text; method-level structures adapted (with attribution above) from Smithsonian territory mapping, Baylor probe design, McAdams' scene scaffold, Crovitz cue logic, and Fisher–Geiselman context reinstatement. No question list from StoryCorps, McAdams, Aron, or any other source was copied in whole or in part.

## Coverage gaps consciously left (see summary)

- No prompts *demanding* grief, loss, medical error, or discrimination narratives — by design (P9); users who want to volunteer such stories can do so via open "moving" invitations.
- Religion/spirituality is reachable through culture/tradition/celebration prompts but has no dedicated territory (high variance in applicant comfort; interviewers rarely probe it).
- Romantic-relationship prompts are limited to the spouse_partner whoDetail and kept mild; dating stories are rarely interview-usable.
- No "greatest achievement / biggest weakness" style prompts — deliberately excluded as life-script bait (P1, P6); the wizard is for material such questions fail to surface.
